Measurement · September 2026

The WooCommerce plugin is published: what the review asked for, and what we measured to answer it

Heurix's WooCommerce plugin has been live on wordpress.org since September 28, 2026. What matters in this release is not the announcement: it is that the review asked questions we could not answer with a measurement, and that two of our three compatibility bounds were higher than necessary with nothing to back them.

What is live, and what that date rests on

The listing is wordpress.org/plugins/heurix-search-for-woocommerce, version 0.2.0. It installs from the WordPress admin by searching for “Heurix Search”. Until today our WooCommerce page said “extension in beta, provided on request”: that was true yesterday, and it was corrected the same day, along with the five other pages of this site that carried the same sentence.

The date comes from one place only: revision r3716469 of the wordpress.org SVN repository, 2026-09-28 09:16:50 +0200, “Adding Heurix Search for WooCommerce by heurix”. It is the only date we can produce, which is why it is the one written everywhere.

The rest of this article covers four things: two bounds lowered because they were wrong, a header added at the request of the review knowing what it costs, and two admin notices that stay non-dismissible.

The WooCommerce bound: 7.0 announced, 6.1 measured

Until September 22, 2026, the plugin declared WC requires at least: 7.0 and WC tested up to: 9.0. Neither line rested on anything: no version of WooCommerce had ever been run. A bound that is too high has no symptom — it excludes shops, silently, and nobody comes to report it.

Measured on September 23, 2026, on WordPress 7.1.2, fifteen versions of WooCommerce from 4.9.5 to 11.1.2, twelve products per trial, a real product search inside a real WooCommerce loop:

WooCommerceposts_per_page as the plugin reads itPage 2Holds
4.9.5, 5.9.2, 6.0.0100 — whatever the Customizer asks foris_search false, found_posts 0, no idsno
6.1.0 to 11.1.2 (twelve versions)16 by default, 5 when the Customizer says so, 100 when it asks for 120found_posts 12, the next sliceyes

The mechanism: on WooCommerce 6.0 and older, WC_Query sets nothing for this query. The plugin reads 0, falls back to its own ceiling and asks for a hundred ids; loop_shop_per_page has no effect, and the second page comes out empty. 6.0.0 does not hold, 6.1.0 does. The bound is measured, not chosen.

What this means on a shop running 6.0 or older: Heurix search shows its first page and returns an empty second one. The plugin does not detect this — it does not read the WooCommerce version, only whether it is present.

The same measurement settled an open question in our code along the way: WC_Query::pre_get_posts is rank 1 and Heurix_Search::intercepter_recherche rank 2, both at priority 10, in all fifteen versions — and this does not depend on the plugin's folder name, verified with heurix-search (loaded before WooCommerce) and zz-heurix-search (loaded after).

Not measured: WooCommerce 3.9.5, which stops on a PHP error before reaching the measurement; the intermediate versions of each branch; any WordPress other than 7.1.2 and any PHP other than 8.5.10.

The WordPress bound: 7.1 announced, 5.9 measured, and why not 5.8

On September 22 the header was brought back to what had actually been run: Requires at least: 7.1. On the 23rd, nine couples lower, it came down to 5.9.

Nine WordPress / WooCommerce couples under PHP 8.1.34, each WordPress paired with the most recent WooCommerce that accepts it — what a merchant would have — archives verified against their published checksum: 5.9.18+6.1.0, 5.9.18+7.0.0, 6.0.16+7.0.0, 6.1.14+7.9.2, 6.3.12+7.9.2, 6.5.12+8.9.5, 6.7.9+9.9.7, 6.9.9+10.9.4, 6.9.9+6.1.0. All nine hold.

5.8.17 and 5.6.21 do not start, and it is not the plugin. mysqli throws by default since PHP 8.1, and their wp-db.php does not set mysqli_report( MYSQLI_REPORT_OFF ). The measurement returns Uncaught mysqli_sql_exception: Table 'wp.wp_options' doesn't exist before installation even finishes. That line appears in WordPress 5.9, with WordPress's own comment naming PHP 8.1. It is WordPress that sets our lower bound, not our code.

What is not measured, and what makes it unable to happen on a merchant's site. WordPress 5.8 and earlier under a PHP they accept — they go down to 5.6.20 — would need a PHP 7.x, ruled out on September 23: the Homebrew formula has been dead since 2022, it would mean a third-party tap and one more php-fpm. That gap cannot open on a shop, because WordPress refuses to activate a plugin whose Requires PHP is not satisfied, and ours asks for 8.1. Verified both ways, by setting Requires PHP: 9.9 in a copy:

“Current versions of WordPress (6.9.9) and PHP (8.1.34) do not meet
  minimum requirements [...] requires WordPress 7.1 and PHP 9.9.”

Two things have to be said for the record to be readable. First: to go below the bound the plugin declared, the bench neutralised the plugin's own Requires at least — without that, WordPress refuses activation and the measurement goes nowhere. Second: Requires PHP: 8.1 remains a precautionary bound. 8.1.34 and 8.5.10 are exercised, nothing below. It is the only one of the three that has never been taken down, and it holds the whole thing up.

A parser detail, for anyone comparing the listing with the header. Tested up to declares 7.1; what ran is 7.1.2. Plugin Check 2.1.0, the official wordpress.org tool, rejects the third number — invalid_tested_upto_minor, an ERROR, measured on this plugin on September 23, 2026. The public listing, for its part, displays “Tested up to 7.1.2”.

Requires Plugins: WordPress resolves by folder name

The wordpress.org review asked for the dependency header. It is enforced from WordPress 6.5; our lower bound is 5.9. What earlier versions do with it is written nowhere in the official documentation: “ignored in silence” was, for us, an inference from reading the source, and that inference was what the question “can we remove the class_exists( 'WooCommerce' ) guard?” depended on.

Measured on September 27, 2026 on three WordPress versions, with wp-cli 2.12.0 and a witness plugin that carries nothing but the header:

5.9.186.4.57.1.2
RequiresPlugins key in get_plugins()absentabsentpresent
WP_Plugin_Dependenciesdoes not existdoes not existexists
Activation, WooCommerce absentsucceededsucceededrefused
The witness's code runsyesyesno

Below 6.5 the header therefore reaches no code at all: no activation, and no display on the Plugins screen. The answer to the open question is no — the guard stays.

And here is what the header costs. WP_Plugin_Dependencies::convert_to_slug() is dirname( $plugin_file ): the dependency is resolved by the name of the installed folder, not by WooCommerce. On WordPress 7.1.2, the same WooCommerce activated from a folder with another name returns:

has_unmet_dependencies                 = true
get_dependency_filepath('woocommerce') = false
class_exists('WooCommerce')            = PRESENT

In other words: if your WooCommerce is not in a folder called woocommerce, WordPress refuses to activate our plugin even though WooCommerce is running and the plugin would work. That is the directory's rule, not an incompatibility; renaming the folder is enough. We added this header knowing it: it is this plugin's third bound higher than necessary, after the two that have just come down, and the first one added on purpose.

What it does not cost, also measured: a plugin that is already active is not deactivated when its dependency stops being satisfied. Updating to a version carrying this header cannot turn your search off.

Both predicates therefore stay, because they do not say the same thing: the header asks for “a folder named woocommerce, installed and active”, the code guard asks for “the WooCommerce class defined”. The two were measured in disagreement, both ways, on the same day.

One honest note about this measurement: the witness's woocommerce folder holds a five-line stand-in whose entire content is class WooCommerce {}. It is not WooCommerce, and that is deliberate: the object being measured is WP_Plugin_Dependencies. The conclusion is about what WordPress resolves, and about nothing else.

The two notices that do not close

The review flagged a “hijack the admin dashboard” pattern — wordpress.org's eleventh guideline, which asks that a plugin's notices stay limited in scope. The flag came from automatic detection, with no named target. To the letter, none of our three notices broke the rule. But one of them came out everywhere: measured on September 27, 2026 on a real WordPress 7.1.2 with WooCommerce absent, the “WooCommerce is not active” notice showed on six screens — dashboard, general settings, post editing, plugins, updates, and user editing.

It is now on the Plugins screen and only there. That is the only screen where the merchant can act, and the notice erases itself there as soon as WooCommerce is active: exactly what the second bullet of the rule asks for — dismissible or self-dismiss when resolved — without storing a dismissal whose key would then decide everything.

What the restriction costs, and why it is acceptable. A merchant who activates Heurix, does not activate WooCommerce and leaves the Plugins page will never learn it again. But in that state the plugin does nothing: it exits before loading a single class, and native WooCommerce search serves, untouched. The cost is “plugin inert in silence”, not “search down”.

The two refusal notices were not touched, and they do not close. They have been contextual since September 19, 2026: they only show on product screens, where the merchant saves products — which the first bullet of the same rule allows explicitly.

  • The indexing refusal. Saving a product cannot fail because of a Heurix refusal: without this notice, the merchant would not know the product was not indexed. It falls by itself at the next accepted upload.
  • The search refusal. It is the only signal that Heurix is serving no search at all — native search takes back over, the shop stays usable, and nothing shows. It carries the number of refused searches, the first and last date, and it is reset from the settings page once the cause is fixed.

Making them dismissible would mean storing a per-user dismissal; that dismissal would then decide whether the merchant sees, or does not see, that their engine has stopped answering. We prefer a notice that stays until the cause is gone. The difference between the two cases is that one, and it reads in the words of the rule rather than in our preference: the first notice names a state the merchant fixes with one click on the screen they are already on; the other two name a state that lasts until a repair elsewhere.

What we did not measure

  • Relevance on a WooCommerce shop. No figure, and not by omission: the bench wires the plugin to a fake Heurix that returns ids in a known order. It measures the WordPress loop, not the ranking. The counts in our measurement of PrestaShop's native search are PrestaShop's, on queries we chose, and that article already refuses to publish them as a rate.
  • A real shop. As of today, the wordpress.org listing shows “fewer than 10 active installations”. Nothing written here comes from a merchant.
  • PHP below 8.1, and a product's variations searched separately (colour, size): in 0.2.0 it is the parent product that is indexed.
  • The integration time. The “3 to 4 days” on our WooCommerce page is an estimate, for a catalog already exported, settings included. Nothing backs that figure on a real integration.

Redoing the measurement

The kit is published in this site's public repository: docs/mesures/woocommerce-bornes-2026-09. It holds the three records cited here and the script for the dependency-header measurement, copied byte for byte.

What it does not redo, and its README says so: the two JSON records come from a bench that is not published — it measures the plugin's circuit breaker first, which has nothing to do with this article. The protocol is described; writing it is still on you. And mesure.sh, which replays entirely, needs 86.5 MB you download yourself (three WordPress releases, wp-cli 2.12.0, the official SQLite drop-in), a PHP 8.1 for WordPress 5.9, and a MariaDB for the versions the SQLite drop-in does not accept.

On your own shop, two checks are worth the trouble before installing anything: your WooCommerce version — below 6.1, the second page of results will be empty — and the name of WooCommerce's folder in wp-content/plugins/, which must be woocommerce on WordPress 6.5 and later.

The rest is documentation: the WooCommerce page, the API documentation, and pricing — the plugin is free and none of its features is held back for a paid version; what you pay for is the service.
