Why "Heurix"? A bit of Greek, a bit of Gaulish

The root of the name doesn't mean "to search." It means "to find." That's the whole difference — and it was more accidental than inspired.

The Greek root: εὑρίσκω

Heur- comes from the Greek verb heuriskō — "I find, I discover." You already know it through two words:

  • Heuristic — a method that helps you find, without guaranteeing the optimal solution. Exactly what a search engine does: it doesn't prove, it approximates.
  • Eureka — heurēka, "I have found," the past tense of the same verb. Attributed to Archimedes stepping out of his bath, in an anecdote reported by Vitruvius two centuries after the fact — which invites some caution.

The detail that matters: finding, not searching

Greek clearly distinguishes zētein, to search — the action, the effort, the process — from heuriskein, to find, the result.

That's precisely the distinction we care about. A naive search engine searches: it compares characters, runs a query, returns a list. It does its job even when the right product isn't in it.

A useful engine finds. When a customer types M8-20 screw stainless and a product is named M8X20 A2, searching isn't enough — these two strings share almost nothing. You have to understand that "M8" denotes a diameter, that "20" is a length, and that "stainless" and "A2" refer to the same material.

That's what our annotation cascade does, and the name says so — even though it wasn't planned.

And the suffix?

-ix is a Latin ending, but in France it immediately evokes Gaulish names as transcribed by the Romans: Vercingetorix, Ambiorix, Dumnorix. And, for most of us, Asterix and Obelix well before the others.

A delightful detail: in those names, -rix means "king." The word is related to the Latin rex and the Sanskrit rāj — the same Indo-European root. Vercingetorix roughly translates to "great king of warriors."

We obviously make no claim to royalty. But a name that sounds both learned and grounded, in an industry where every search engine is called something-ly or something-ify, didn't strike us as a bad thing.

Honesty about the etymology

It has to be said: this explanation is largely reconstructed after the fact. The name was first chosen because it sounded good, was short, pronounceable in both French and English, and the domain was available — three criteria that matter a great deal when launching a product.

The coherence only showed up afterward. Which, for a brand, isn't really a flaw: the names that work are often the ones whose meaning you discover later.

How to say it

"HYOOR-iks," like heuristic. The "h" isn't silent by accident — it's there as an inheritance from Greek, where the rough breathing of εὑρίσκω was transcribed as an h in Latin.

If you say "HER-iks," no one will correct you. Neither will we.
